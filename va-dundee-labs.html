Draft scope · Design for Scotland living labs

Scoping living labs for the next ten years

Prepared for V&A Dundee by Dark Matter Labs · November 2026 · For discussion

“A schoolroom for everyone.” Henry Cole, first Director of the V&A, on the museum’s purpose

Purpose of this scope

Design for Scotland is the underlying endeavour. Living labs are the mechanism through which it meets the world.

This scope builds on V&A Dundee’s Phase 3 proposal for Design for Scotland and on recent conversations between V&A Dundee and Dark Matter Labs. It proposes that Design for Scotland forms the base layer: the philosophy, evidence, networks and advocacy built over four years, and the long-term case for a National Design Policy.

On that foundation, V&A Dundee would open a series of living labs. Each lab takes the endeavour into a specific place and question that matters now, works with the people already there, and brings what it learns back into the base layer. Labs open as new needs emerge and close when their work is complete. The endeavour itself continues, and stays relevant because its labs follow what the world needs at any given moment.

Two labs are proposed to begin: a Scotland Bioregioning Lab and a Human Futures Lab. Dark Matter Labs would develop both in partnership with V&A Dundee.

Design for Scotland as the base layer

The Phase 3 proposal makes a strong case. Four years of Design for Scotland have built the evidence, networks and national ambition for a more strategic approach to design. The proposal names the right pressures: AI and automation, strain on universities and cultural institutions, and the need for productive, sustainable growth. It places V&A Dundee at the intersection of design, culture, research and industry, with a home in the UK’s first UNESCO City of Design.

It also secures a regional base. Through Tay Create, the Tay Cities region’s UKRI-backed innovation partnership worth up to £20 million, V&A Dundee will lead the design programme for creative technologies.

In this model, the base layer holds what stays constant across every lab:

  • Philosophy and method. Design as a national capability for understanding complex problems, bringing different voices together and testing possibilities before committing resources.
  • Network. The designers, art schools, universities, industry partners and creative communities that Design for Scotland has gathered.
  • Advocacy. The case for a National Design Policy for Scotland, increasingly evidenced by what the labs achieve.
  • Investment in designers. Paid commissions, residencies and placements that put money directly into Scotland’s design sector.

The first two labs carry forward Phase 3’s commitments to the design economy and to future skills, and give each a place where it can be seen working.

How the labs work

As drafted, Phase 3 frames its work as applying design research to Scotland’s shared challenges. That framing suits advocacy and policy. Funders, partners and communities are more often persuaded by visible change in places they know. The labs would follow six principles:

  • Start from a live need. Each lab begins with a need that is pressing now, in a named place, with people already working on it.
  • Work with those already doing it. Labs join existing efforts, such as Bioregioning Tayside, and add design capacity to them.
  • Pay for work on the ground. Each paid commission is attached to a real project with a named partner.
  • Return and record. Labs revisit their work after three and six months and record what changed. That record flows back into the base layer as evidence.
  • Open and close with need. Each lab has a defined purpose and a review point. When the need shifts, the lab adapts or closes, and new labs can open.
  • Share people. A small core team, fixed-term tenures from partner organisations, and student placements from art schools across Scotland, Wales, Ireland and England, all able to move between labs.
Lab one

Scotland Bioregioning Lab

Purpose: to put design to work in the economy of Scotland’s places, and to give the bioregioning movement shared capacity to sense need, coordinate activity and share learning.

Context

Phase 3 aims to strengthen Scotland’s design economy and open new markets for design expertise. One of the largest emerging markets is regeneration in place: restoring land, water and local economies. Bioregioning organises this work around living places, such as watersheds and firths, and some of the most significant practice in Europe is close to V&A Dundee.

  • Bioregioning Tayside ↗, a community-led initiative active since 2020, is developing the Tayside Bioregional Financing Facility ↗ with Dark Matter Labs. Work to date includes a regional stocktake, a bioregional plan, an open map of regeneration projects and trials of participatory micro-granting.
  • The Findhorn Watershed Initiative ↗ is restoring a river system from the Monadhliath Mountains to the Moray Firth.
  • RZ Studio ↗, part of the Dark Matter Labs group, supports place-based teams to build their own capital allocation capacity.

Grounded in practice

The pilot’s paid design commissions would go to projects already on Bioregioning Tayside’s map: farms, food enterprises, river restoration and community land projects that need help with services, business models, communication or investment readiness. Designers gain new clients, and projects gain capability. Over time, the lab would provide what Leon Seefeld of Dark Matter Labs describes as “cascading risk sensing capabilities”, “local coordination capacity” and “comparative learning” for bioregions across Scotland. Within bioregioning practice, this is often described as a lighthouse function.

“A Bioregional Financing Facility is not built—it is grown in dialogue with place.” Leon Seefeld, They Say Art Unlocks New Perspectives ↗

Relationship with Eden Project Dundee

Eden Project Dundee is planned for the former gasworks on East Dock Street, less than a mile from V&A Dundee. Its backers have shown strong interest in bioregioning, and the visitor experience is intended to present Eden as part of the bioregion it occupies. A bioregioning lab at V&A Dundee would allow the field, the network and the public narrative to develop before Eden breaks ground, establishing a working relationship between the two institutions from the outset and a clear point of handover once Eden opens.

Resourcing
Nature and place-based funders, bioregional finance, enterprise agencies, and Eden’s backers.
Partners
Bioregioning Tayside, the Findhorn watershed, and emerging bioregions across Scotland.
Evidence of success
Projects strengthened, designers in new markets, and a national network able to sense, coordinate and learn.
Lab two

Human Futures Lab

Purpose: to explore what it means to be human in an age shaped by AI, and to help Dundee’s people find new and thriving roles.

Context

Phase 3 calls for the capabilities Scotland needs in an era of AI: making, craft, critical thinking, and creative and emotional intelligence. Dundee is where that need is most immediate. The city’s economic history is tied to the skills of its people, from jute, jam and journalism to games and design. That foundation is under pressure. According to the University of Dundee, its staff headcount fell by 675 between 1 August 2024 and 1 May 2026, and further reductions have been proposed since. The effect on civic confidence extends well beyond the university.

“What does it mean to be human now? I think this is the foundational challenge.” Indy Johar, Dark Matter Labs, on The Great Simplification ↗

In conversation with Nate Hagens, Indy Johar argues that machine capabilities mean “the human contribution to the economy as a bad robot is no longer sufficient”. He frames this as an opportunity to broaden our understanding of human contribution, including care, situated intelligence and the “freedom to be radically human”.

Grounded in practice

  • A schoolroom for everyone. Open studios at V&A Dundee where residents, including people leaving contracting institutions, learn making, craft and AI tools side by side with designers.
  • Prototype roles. Work with employers, colleges and art schools to design and test new roles and routes into work, starting with people in Dundee who are changing careers now.
  • Futures in the galleries. Exhibit scenarios for work, learning and care in Dundee over the next fifteen years, built from what the studios and prototypes reveal.
Resourcing
Foundations focused on the future of work, learning and AI, alongside skills and regeneration funding.
Partners
Local government, colleges and universities, employers, and residents in transition.
Evidence of success
People in new roles, new routes into work, and a city with a renewed account of its own future.

How this shapes the 12-month pilot

The Phase 3 pilot structure holds. The two labs become the focus of the pilot’s practical work.

  1. Establish the base layer and lab model · months 1–3. Alongside the advisory group and partner mapping, agree the places, partners and live needs each of the first two labs will work on.
  2. Open the first two labs · months 3–9. Paid commissions with Tayside regeneration projects, and open studios and role prototypes in Dundee.
  3. Convene a National Design Summit · months 9–10. Present the work through the projects and people involved, so that the case for a National Design Policy rests on visible results.
  4. Build the next three to five years · months 10–12. A strategy for Design for Scotland as the base layer, a business case for each lab with its own funders, and a process for opening further labs as new needs emerge.

Why this structure

Dark Matter Labs has grown as a family of labs, each with its own mission, funders and partners, supported by shared people, methods and back-office functions. The same structure would serve Design for Scotland well:

  • Relevance. Labs follow what is needed now. The endeavour stays current without changing its foundations.
  • Resilience. No single funding stream carries the whole programme, matching the blended funding approach Phase 3 already proposes.
  • Focus. Each lab is accountable to a clear purpose and to its own funders.
  • Public engagement. V&A Dundee makes the work visible through exhibitions, events and the building itself.

Proposed next steps

  1. Working session. A half-day in Dundee with V&A Dundee and Dark Matter Labs to test this scope against the Phase 3 proposal.
  2. Partners on the ground. Early conversations with Bioregioning Tayside, the Tay Create partners, and Dundee employers and colleges.
  3. Lab briefs. A short brief for each of the first two labs covering purpose, resourcing, partners and first-year work, to support the pilot and later funding conversations.
  4. Early engagement with Eden. Open the bioregioning conversation with the Eden Project team and its backers while the relationship is still forming.

Dark Matter Labs would welcome the opportunity to develop this scope further with V&A Dundee, and to share what it has learned from building labs of its own.
